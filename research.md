# Research | Jason Dunn

### Working Papers

* **Ghost Towns and Big Cities: Historical Mining Districts and Economic Activity in the American West** with <a href="https://jamessiodla.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">James Siodla</a><br>(Forthcoming at the _Journal of Economic History_)
  <div style="margin-top: 5px;">
    <a href="assets/mining.pdf" target="_blank" class="paper-btn">Paper</a>
    <a href="assets/mining_appendix.pdf" target="_blank" class="paper-btn">Online Appendix</a>
    <details style="margin-top: 4px;">
      <summary style="cursor: pointer; color: #4A148C; font-weight: bold;">
        Abstract
      </summary>
      <div style="margin-top: 5px; font-style: italic; font-size: 0.95em;">
        This paper identifies the impact of gold and silver mining discoveries in the American West on the origins and death rates of western towns and the region's long-run population distribution. Between 1850 and 1940, the discovery of mining sites increased the likelihood that a town formed nearby relative to other locations. Many of these towns eventually died, however, due largely to their relatively poor geography. Nevertheless, locations near mining sites exhibit conditional persistence: they are denser today than surrounding areas, but only when accounting for geographic confounders. Our findings suggest that historical mining activity influenced the locations of cities and towns in the American West and geography helped determine their long-run prospects for survival.
      </div>
    </details>
  </div>

* **Navigating the Waves of Global Shipping: Drivers and Aggregate Implications** <br>with <a href="https://www.fernandoleibovici.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Fernando Leibovici</a><br>(Revise & Resubmit at the _American Economic Review_)
  <div style="margin-top: 5px;">
    <a href="assets/shipping.pdf" target="_blank" class="paper-btn">Paper</a>
    <a href="assets/shipping_appendix.pdf" target="_blank" class="paper-btn">Online Appendix</a>
    <details style="margin-top: 4px;">
      <summary style="cursor: pointer; color: #4A148C; font-weight: bold;">
        Abstract
      </summary>
      <div style="margin-top: 5px; font-style: italic; font-size: 0.95em;">
        This paper studies the drivers of global shipping dynamics and their aggregate implications. We document novel evidence on the dynamics of global shipping supply, demand, and costs. Motivated by this evidence, we set up a dynamic model of international trade with a global shipping market where shipping firms and importers endogenously determine shipping supply and costs. We find the model accounts for the dynamics of global shipping observed at business cycle frequencies and in the aftermath of COVID-19. Accounting for global shipping significantly affects the dynamics of aggregate economic activity.
      </div>
    </details>
  </div>

* **Depressed in School: The Great Depression, Educational Attainment, and Productivity Growth**
  <div style="margin-top: 5px;">
    <a href="assets/depressed.pdf" target="_blank" class="paper-btn">Paper</a>
    <a href="assets/depressed_slides.pdf" target="_blank" class="paper-btn">Slides</a>
    <details style="margin-top: 4px;">
      <summary style="cursor: pointer; color: #4A148C; font-weight: bold;">
        Abstract
      </summary>
      <div style="margin-top: 5px; font-style: italic; font-size: 0.95em;">
        While the Great Depression caused severe immediate declines in employment and output, this paper studies a positive additional consequence: increased human capital accumulation. To identify this, I construct a granular shift-share instrument for county exposure to youth unemployment, using 8 sectors, 47 manufacturing industries, and 8 demographic subgroups. Counties more exposed to the Depression saw greater increases in high school attainment rates. By 1940, this shock-induced education sorted these cohorts into higher-skilled occupations. Harder-hit counties experienced greater increases in the education of their workforce and labor productivity. These findings suggest that although highly exposed counties were negatively affected from the initial shock of the Depression, it induced educational attainment and put them on the trajectory for higher growth in the long run.
      </div>
    </details>
  </div>

* **Spatial Wage Estimates for 19th Century US Workers**
  <div style="margin-top: 5px;">
    <a href="assets/spatialwages.pdf" target="_blank" class="paper-btn">Paper</a>
    <a href="assets/spatialwages_slides.pdf" target="_blank" class="paper-btn">Slides</a>
    <details style="margin-top: 4px;">
      <summary style="cursor: pointer; color: #4A148C; font-weight: bold;">
        Abstract
      </summary>
      <div style="margin-top: 5px; font-style: italic; font-size: 0.95em;">
        Measuring socioeconomic status (SES) throughout US history is challenging due to the (i) lack of direct income data and (ii) limitations of occupation scores, which ignore geographic and demographic variation. While recent literature has improved upon these scores by accounting for education, wealth, and other SES factors, they remain coarse or are limited by incomplete reporting thresholds. This paper's core contribution is developing a method for tightly estimating individual manufacturing wages from 1850 to 1880. I supplement these with broader wages on non-manufacturing occupations for greater coverage of the 19th century workforce. I then set up a procedure for benchmarking these wage estimates and other SES measures against external SES sources. I find the wage data and a composite index of wages, literacy and wealth perform at or above pre-existing SES measures. This new SES measure implies lower levels of economic mobility and greater increases in inequality over the course of the 19th century.
      </div>
    </details>
  </div>
